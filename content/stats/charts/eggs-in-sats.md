## What moves this line

Two prices are hiding in this one line: what a dozen eggs costs in dollars, and what a dollar is worth in sats. The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the first each month from store surveys in U.S. cities. The second comes from the average bitcoin price over the same month. Divide one by the other and you get the price of eggs for someone who keeps their savings in sats.

Most of the fall since 2011 comes from the second price. In August 2016 eggs cost $1.46 a dozen and a dollar bought about 173,000 sats, so a dozen came to roughly 251,000 sats. In August 2026 eggs cost $2.27, more in dollars, yet a dollar bought about 1,450 sats, so the same dozen came to 3,294 sats. Eggs did not get cheaper; sats got more expensive, by far more than eggs did.

The egg price still shows through, though. The dollar spikes from bird flu in 2015, early 2023, and early 2025 all appear here as bumps, as long as the bitcoin price was not moving faster in the opposite direction at the same time. In January 2023 eggs hit $4.82 while bitcoin was near a low, so the sats price jumped. In March 2025 eggs reached $6.23, a higher dollar price than in 2023, yet in sats that bump was less than a third as tall, because a dollar bought so many fewer sats by then.

The point of this chart is not that eggs are a good investment or that bitcoin is. It is that a price always has two sides, and measuring the same carton in a different unit changes the story completely. [Bitcoin and inflation](https://fastfactsforsats.com/money/bitcoin-and-inflation/) on Fast Facts takes that idea further, and [Everyday prices in sats](/value/everyday-prices/) does the same calculation for gasoline, milk, coffee, and the rest of the basket this month.
