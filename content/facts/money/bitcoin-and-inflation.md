Bitcoin and inflation are often discussed together.

The basic argument sounds simple:

Dollars can be created. Bitcoin has a limit.

That part is true.

The conclusion is more complicated.

Bitcoin's supply cannot be inflated beyond its rules, but its dollar price can move dramatically in either direction.

## Two different systems

The U.S. Consumer Price Index tracks changes in the cost of goods and services.

The CPI was 220.2 in January 2011 and [[live:cpi]].

That means dollars lost purchasing power during that period.

Bitcoin's supply followed a very different path.

New bitcoin is created according to a published schedule.

Today, roughly 450 new bitcoin are issued each day.

After the next [halving](/money/halvings/), that falls roughly in half again.

Eventually, new issuance stops.

## Does that make bitcoin an inflation hedge?

Over long periods, bitcoin has dramatically outperformed U.S. inflation.

But short periods tell a different story.

In 2022, U.S. inflation remained high while bitcoin's dollar price fell sharply.

Someone who bought bitcoin simply to avoid one year of inflation could have lost far more in bitcoin's price decline than inflation cost them.

That matters.

## Fixed supply and stable price are not the same thing

Bitcoin's supply rule is stable.

Bitcoin's market price is not.

Those are two different ideas.

Its price responds to demand, interest rates, regulation, sentiment, economic conditions, and many other factors.

A fixed supply does not remove volatility.

## So what does the fixed supply actually do?

It gives Bitcoin a known monetary policy.

You can know approximately how many new bitcoin will exist next year.

And ten years from now.

And decades from now.

That predictability is unusual.

Whether the market eventually rewards it is another question entirely.

Scarcity sets the stage. Demand decides whether anyone buys a ticket.

## Read the charts carefully

Long-term charts and short-term charts answer different questions.

A decade can show one trend.

A single year can show the opposite.

[Fast Stats](https://faststatsforsats.com/charts/) lets you compare both: the [sats per dollar chart](https://faststatsforsats.com/charts/sats-per-dollar/) for the long view and the [everyday prices in sats](https://faststatsforsats.com/value/everyday-prices/) page for this month.

What happened is measurable.

What happens next is not.
